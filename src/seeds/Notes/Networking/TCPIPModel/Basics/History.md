# From ARPANET to TCP/IP

<hr class="dividerSection" />

### The Origins of ARPANET

<hr class="dividerSection" />

Early work on the computer networks that would evolve into today's internet began in the 1960s.

The <span class="emphasis">US Department of Defense's ARPA</span> (Advanced Research Projects Agency) funded <span class="emphasis">ARPANET</span>, which came online in 1969 to connect mainframes at universities and research labs across the country.

Early ARPANET maps show connections concentrated mostly along the East and West coasts of the United States, linking major universities, research labs, and government sites together.

ARPANET originally used a protocol called <span class="emphasis">NCP</span> (Network Control Program).

### The Development of TCP/IP

<hr class="dividerSection" />

In 1974, <span class="emphasis">Vint Cerf</span> and <span class="emphasis">Bob Kahn</span>, working at DARPA, began developing <span class="emphasis">TCP</span> (Transmission Control Program) as an internetworking protocol.

TCP went through several revisions and was later divided into two protocols still used today:

<div class="centeredBullet">
  <ul class="diamondBullets fullWidthBullet">
    <li><span class="emphasis">Transmission Control Protocol (TCP)</span></li>
    <li><span class="emphasis">Internet Protocol (IP)</span></li>
  </ul>
</div>

Together, these two protocols form the foundation of the protocol suite known as <span class="emphasis">TCP/IP</span> today.

<hr class="dividerSection" />

### ARPANET's Switch to TCP/IP

<hr class="dividerSection" />

ARPANET fully switched to TCP/IP on <span class="emphasis">January 1, 1983</span>, an event often referred to as the ARPANET "flag day."

<hr class="dividerSection" />

### Why TCP/IP Became Dominant

<hr class="dividerSection" />

TCP/IP became dominant over other vendor-proprietary solutions at the time for two main reasons:

<div class="centeredBullet">
  <ul class="diamondBullets fullWidthBullet">
    <li>It was published as a set of <span class="emphasis">open standards</span> that any vendor could implement.</li>
    <li>It could run over many different types of underlying networks.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

Several decades later, TCP/IP remains the foundation of internet communication.
